2.  The market slide (slide 9)
This is the slide that gets challenged. The numbers are defensible, but only if you explain the method before you say the figure. Lead with the method.
2.1  Why we size a fee pool and not a pile of assets
We can charge three different ways, and we probably will charge all three: a flat monthly subscription, a percentage of assets we advise on (AUA), and a licence fee when a distributor or bank runs our engines inside their own product.
That creates a problem for conventional market sizing. If we sized the market as number of investors × our subscription price, the "market" would change every time we changed our pricing. A market that moves when your price list moves is not a market — it is a revenue forecast wearing a costume.
So we size the fee pool instead:
TAM  =  the assets we could advise on  ×  the rate at which advice is actually paid for
Say this out loud in the meeting. It is monetisation-agnostic: whether that rupee of revenue arrives as a subscription, an AUA fee or a licence, it is the same rupee coming out of the same pool. That is the whole argument, and it is a strength, not a hedge.

2.3  The rate — 50 basis points, and 15 on treasury
Why 50 bps. It is the low end of the trail commission paid on a regular-plan mutual fund. That is money Indian investors are already paying, every year, for guidance that comes with a conflict of interest attached. Using it means we are claiming redirected spending, not new spending — a far easier argument than persuading an investor that Indians will start paying for something they have never paid for. It is also one-fifth of SEBI's 2.5% cap on adviser fees, so nobody can call it aggressive.
Why only 15 bps on corporate treasury. Advising a company on where to park its cash earns a fraction of what advising on a portfolio earns. Liquid-fund total expense ratios themselves run 10–20 bps, so 15 is the honest number. Pricing treasury at the full 50 bps would have added roughly ₹10,000 Cr to TAM for no defensible reason — and an investor who knows the space would have caught it.

2.7  The crossover — ₹15.8 lakh
A flat ₹7,900 a year equals 50 bps of assets on a portfolio of ₹15.8 lakh (₹7,900 ÷ 0.005). Below that, the flat subscription earns us more than an AUA fee would. Above it, AUA earns more. Institutions get SaaS licensing, which is uncapped by portfolio size.
Why this matters in the room: it turns "your pricing model is unproven" into "we have three ways to monetise the same pool, and we know exactly where each one wins". If an investor pushes on subscription fatigue, this is your answer.

3.  What we left out, and why
The footnote on slide 9 reads "Promoter stakes, institutions and PMS excluded." Each of those three is a deliberate deduction, and each one makes our number smaller. Being able to explain them is what makes the rest of the slide credible.
3.1  Promoter stakes — 50.0% of the market, gone
Half of the value of every company listed on the NSE is held by its founders, their families or its parent company. Those are control blocks. They are not traded, not rebalanced, and never the subject of portfolio advice — a promoter who sold down would be making a corporate decision, not an investment one.
So: we count 9.6% + 1.9% + 2.9% = 14.4% of NSE market cap as addressable, and leave the remaining 85.6% out. That removes about ₹231 lakh crore of promoter holdings alone.
3.2  Institutions — foreign funds, banks and insurers
Foreign portfolio investors hold 17.3% of the market; domestic banks, insurers and other institutions hold another 5.5%. EPFO is excluded too. These are organisations with in-house research desks, Bloomberg terminals and sell-side coverage. They are not going to buy a ₹1,499-a-month diagnosis, and pretending otherwise would be obvious.
The one institutional-looking pool we do keep is corporate treasury — ordinary companies parking cash in mutual funds — and we discount it heavily, both in rate (15 bps) and in reach (40% in SAM).
3.3  PMS — the one that looks like a mistake but is not
SEBI reports ₹44.1 lakh crore under portfolio managers, of which ₹31.9 lakh crore is EPFO. The remaining ₹9.03 lakh crore looks like a large addressable pool sitting right there in the statistics — and we removed all of it.
The reason: in India, a PMS does not hold your shares. Your shares sit in your own demat account and the portfolio manager has a power of attorney to trade them. So every rupee of PMS assets is already counted in our direct-equity line. Adding PMS would count the same shares twice.
The identical logic removes registered investment adviser AUA and distributor AUA. These are all wrappers over assets we have already counted, not additional assets.
If an investor says "your market looks small for India"
That is the right response to get. Answer: "It is smaller than the headline numbers because we took out promoter control blocks, institutional money and every advisory wrapper that double-counts the underlying assets. What is left is money that can actually be advised. We would rather defend a small honest number than a large one."

6.5  The global-coverage price premium
We advise on globally invested portfolios, and there is evidence that global coverage carries a price premium in India. Trendlyne charges about ₹310 a month for domestic coverage and ₹1,500–2,000 a month for its global tiers — roughly five times. Supporting demand evidence: outward remittances for equity and debt investment under the RBI's Liberalised Remittance Scheme ran at US$1.06 bn in April–June FY27 alone, double the year before, and it is the fastest-growing purpose category in that data. India has about 390,000 dollar-millionaires holding US$1.64 trillion.

The line that earns the slide: because the engines can be licensed as readily as they are used, an infrastructure sale stays open even if the consumer product plateaus. That is a genuine second door, and it is why the slide is worth showing at all.
Internal only — the numbers behind the routes. These are deliberately NOT on the slide.
On ₹12.49 Cr at 20%, diluted to 13.0% after future rounds: a 3.0× return needs a ₹288 Cr exit (24.6% IRR) · 5.0× needs ₹480 Cr (38.0%) · 7.8× needs ₹750 Cr (50.8%) · 10.0× needs ₹961 Cr (58.5%).
Cross-check from the other direction: the base case of ₹62.9 Cr of Year-5 revenue at a 6× revenue multiple implies a ₹377 Cr exit — 3.9×, or a 31.5% IRR. A 10× return would require about 11.9× that Year-5 revenue.
Wealthtech M&A in India has transacted at roughly 8–15× revenue.
If pushed for numbers in a meeting: "The exit sensitivities are in the model — happy to walk you through them separately." Then do. Do not improvise a multiple across the table.
